## domestic_policy

### legislative_capacity_constraint
confidence 0.95, 1 observation

Carney's Liberal government won 169 of 338 seats in April 2025 elections, three short of the 172-seat majority threshold, requiring opposition coalition support to pass major fiscal or regulatory legislation. Mechanism: minority government requires coalition negotiation on every major legislative initiative, slowing decision-making and forcing concessions to opposition priorities. Implication: major supply-side initiatives (interprovincial trade liberalization, tax reform, regulatory streamlining) require opposition buy-in or face legislative blockage. Creates structural constraint on agenda speed and scope relative to majority government. What would change it: byelection win shifts seat count to majority, opposition parties withdraw confidence (forcing new election), or Carney negotiates confidence-and-supply agreement with coalition partner.

### carbon_tax_policy
confidence 0.9, 2 observations

Carney eliminated the federal carbon tax via order-in-council as his first executive action, reversing the Trudeau government's price-based climate instrument. Mechanism: removal increases household disposable income and reduces producer input costs, while eliminating the primary federal price on carbon emissions. B.C. aligned by eliminating the consumer carbon tax while retaining the industrial emitters component, suggesting political pressure or alignment against consumer-facing carbon pricing. The elimination of consumer carbon pricing, paired with acceptance of high sectoral energy tariffs (35% non-CUSMA, 50% steel/aluminum), deferred climate-stringency in project approvals, and deprioritization of fossil-fuel-free infrastructure, reveals ranking: competitiveness and near-term growth >> climate-price policy. What would change it: political pressure to reinstate carbon pricing, fiscal deterioration requiring new revenue sources, or climate crisis escalation forcing return to price-based instruments.

### income_tax_relief
confidence 0.88, 5 observations

Targeted fiscal stimulus for households: lowest income tax bracket reduced 1 percentage point to 14% effective July 1, 2025, generating $840 annual tax savings per two-income family across 22 million Canadian taxpayers. Nearly half (49%) of relief concentrated in lowest bracket ($57,375 and below annual taxable income), directing stimulus to high-marginal-propensity-to-consume households. Payroll tax withholding tables updated July 1, front-loading fiscal relief into H2 2025 rather than deferring to spring 2026 tax filing—accelerating demand impulse during tariff-driven recession risk. Mechanism: lower tax burden on lowest-income households increases disposable income and spending during period of high uncertainty from tariff exposure and trade-policy volatility; front-loaded payroll delivery ensures demand enters Q3-Q4 2025 rather than Q1-Q2 2026, supporting near-term growth during cyclical weakness. Reveals ranking: immediate household income support and demand stabilization >> fiscal consolidation and deficit reduction. What would change it: fiscal deterioration forcing tax relief reversal, resolution of tariff uncertainty eliminating demand-support rationale, or labour-market recovery reducing high-MPC household targeting.

### usmca_renegotiation
confidence 0.88, 6 observations

Renegotiation strategy prioritizes CUSMA preferential-access preservation over headline tariff convergence with EU/Japan baselines. Carney declined to target the 15% tariff achieved by EU and Japan in their recent settlements, instead positioning Canada's geographic proximity and energy leverage (US dependence on Canadian energy supplies) as differentiated negotiating assets that justify accepting higher headline tariff exposure (currently 35% on non-CUSMA goods, 50% on steel/aluminum) to preserve CUSMA preferential access. This reveals a strategic ranking: CUSMA preservation >> tariff-rate parity with other trading blocs. Negotiators downplay Trump's Friday deadline and characterize talks as complex, accepting extended negotiation rather than rushed closure—signalling that Carney will not sacrifice CUSMA terms for deadline compliance. Steel/aluminum sectoral costs (50% global tariff) are accepted without exemption negotiation, consistent with a preservation-of-broader-access strategy. Renegotiation may modernize rules of origin or tighten compliance, but primary objective is defending current CUSMA framework against Trump's asymmetric escalation, not pursuing a structural reset to achieve headline tariff parity. What would change it: substantial deterioration in CUSMA preferential access (new Trump escalation threatening core CUSMA exemption), successful renegotiation achieving lower non-CUSMA rates while protecting CUSMA, or geopolitical realignment restoring integration logic.

## monetary_conditions

### growth_trajectory
confidence 0.88, 13 observations

Canada entered recession-like conditions in Q2 2025, with real GDP contracting 1.6%, driven by sharp export declines (real volume down 9.1%, US-bound exports down 15.7%, auto exports down 17.4%) and deferred business investment. Carney warned Canada faces 'one or two quarters of negative economic growth' and a 'recession-like environment later this year' due to spillover from US tariff escalation. Mechanism: merchandise trade surplus narrowed to $3.6B in April (smallest since Dec 2020), cumulative $15B export loss since start of 2026, business capex pullback amid CUSMA renegotiation and geopolitical tariff threats. This is not a pull-forward reversal or temporary shock—the damage is concentrated in US trade and structural (real volume, not price). Shifts if: US tariff rollback achieved through CUSMA renegotiation, trade uncertainty resolves, or monetary/fiscal stimulus triggers recovery.

### boc_rate_path
confidence 0.85, 7 observations

Easing cycle is complete or near-exhausted. The Bank's October 2025 rate cut to 2.25% is characterized as 'the last in the cycle' by financial markets and BoC guidance. Mechanism: policy is now at the lower end of the neutral range, and policymakers have explicitly stated that 'rate cuts can only do so much to help the economy adjust to structural (trade) challenges.' The signal is clear—no further cuts are anticipated absent recession, and monetary policy alone cannot address structural headwinds (tariffs, regulatory barriers, energy import dependence). The October cut was driven by unemployment rising to 7.1% and employment declines, but the consensus is that labour-market slack requires fiscal or structural-policy response, not further monetary easing. Shifts if: employment deteriorates sharply (recession scenario forcing emergency cuts), inflation unexpectedly re-accelerates above core target, or geopolitical crisis triggers emergency rate adjustments.

### labour_market_deterioration
confidence 0.85, 10 observations

Employment has declined over recent months, with unemployment rising to 7.1%, driven by significant job losses in trade-exposed sectors (auto 17.4% export drop, steel, aluminum, lumber, canola, pork, seafood) and broader pullback in business hiring due to trade-policy uncertainty. Wage growth easing. Mechanism: US tariffs on autos, Chinese tariffs on agricultural products, new US copper tariff, higher US lumber tariff; business capex paused and hiring scaled back amid CUSMA renegotiation uncertainty and geopolitical tariff threats. Trade-policy uncertainty is the dominant brake on hiring, superseding conventional monetary policy signalling. Shifts if: US tariff threats recede, CUSMA renegotiation stabilizes policy outlook, or fiscal/monetary stimulus offsets sectoral losses.

### cad_exchange_rate_drivers
confidence 0.76, 4 observations

Canadian dollar weakness is driven primarily by U.S./Canada short-term interest rate differential: Fed policy rate 4%, BoC policy rate 2.25%, a 175 basis-point gap making the U.S. dollar 'more attractive to investors.' Mechanism: the rate differential is the carry driver for currency weakness. Outlook for 2026 recovery: Scotiabank projects Fed will cut 100 basis points (4%→3%) through 2026, compressing U.S. dollar yield advantage; BoC expected to tighten policy late in 2026, further narrowing differentials. Combined effect should compress the rate gap substantially (from 175bp toward ~50–75bp), lifting CAD. Specific forecast: Scotiabank projects CAD 72.5¢ USD in Q1 2026, appreciating 1¢ per quarter to 75¢ by year-end 2026. CIBC expects 72.5¢ by Q4 2025, recovering from current 71¢. Both banks flag trade-policy risks (tariff escalation, CUSMA renegotiation failure) as potential downside to this appreciation scenario. Shifts if: trade-policy escalation reverses Fed easing expectations, BoC remains on hold longer than expected, or fiscal deterioration requires emergency monetary accommodation.

### labour_market_mobility
confidence 0.65, 2 observations

Reduced labour mobility between sectors is limiting productivity and income growth. Workers staying in low-productivity sectors (unable or unwilling to transition) due to hiring freezes, geopolitical uncertainty, and rising skill requirements for open positions. Mechanism: interest rate spikes (2022-23) combined with tariff/geopolitical uncertainty caused businesses to cut hiring rather than lay off workers, reducing job churn and dynamism. With hiring frozen, workers cannot move to higher-productivity opportunities; firms, fearing further shocks, are not hiring. Outcome: 'Workers aren't moving from less productive sectors to more productive ones as much, which slows down Canada's productivity, income growth and collective purchasing power.' This structural rigidity persists even as immediate trade shocks are absorbed, creating a labour-supply efficiency loss that monetary policy cannot address. Shifts if: hiring resumes and labour churn normalizes, tariff uncertainty resolves, or sectoral diversification (away from trade-exposed sectors) creates reallocation pathways.
