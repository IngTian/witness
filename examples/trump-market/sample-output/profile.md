# Trump Administration, Second Term: Market Impact and Policy Constraint Arc

The administration's signature second-term moves—tariff escalation to 17–18% average (highest since 1934) and Iran war authorization—jointly produced a 4.2% trailing inflation (May 2026), the outcome the administration was simultaneously working hardest to prevent. This self-inflicted constraint reveals the operative hierarchy: tariff escalation and military assertion ranked higher than the monetary accommodation required for the stated 8–12% GDP growth target.

The inflation transmission ran through two channels. Tariff authority raised imported-goods costs; Iran's Strait of Hormuz closure (disrupting ~20% of global petroleum flows) triggered the "largest energy supply disruption in modern history," driving energy prices sharply higher. Fed economists assessed the Iran shock as "considerably more impactful" than tariffs alone. The result: the Fed shifted decisively from rate-pause stance to rate-hiking cycle, ending November 2025 market expectations of 87% odds for a December rate cut and instead committing to rate increases into 3.9% by September 2026. The administration had created precisely the monetary tightening it sought to avoid.

Equity markets internalized this constraint early and adopted explicit TACO pricing—"Trump Always Chickens Out"—following April 2025 and January 2026 reversals. When the 30% EU tariff threat triggered an intraday Treasury-yield spike (~80bp), Trump reversed course within hours, announcing cancellation and triggering a synchronized equity rally (S&P +1.16%, Dow +1.21%). This pattern repeated enough that equity investors stopped repricing to tariff announcements; July 28, 2025 EU trade-deal announcement moved the S&P 500 just +0.02%, with market commentary explicitly deprioritizing tariffs: "Right now, we're presuming these tariffs don't matter. What matters is earnings, unemployment, the Fed meeting, and—you know what—dead last, tariffs." Headline tariff rates became unreliable signals; investors priced the expected negotiated outcome, not the announced outcome.

Below headline rates, supply-chain reconfiguration accelerated irreversibly. China shifted soybean purchases to Brazil; the EU opened membership discussions for CPTPP, a trade bloc excluding the U.S.; corporate supply chains restructured away from tariff-exposed geographies. Even if tariffs reverse, these relocations persist—repatriation fixed costs are high, and alternative supplier networks have years of implementation lag. The PIIE assessment: "The US share of world trade will decline somewhat," a durable structural shift independent of tariff-rate reversals.

The administration's most explicit objective conflict surfaced over Fed independence. On January 11, 2026, DOJ served Federal Reserve subpoenas threatening criminal indictment against Chair Powell, explicitly tied to his decision to set rates on economic conditions rather than presidential preference. Powell stated plainly: "The threat of criminal charges is a consequence of the Federal Reserve setting interest rates based on our best assessment of what will serve the public, rather than following the preferences of the President." June 2026: Supreme Court blocked Trump's removal attempt of Fed Governor Lisa Cook on due-process grounds; simultaneously, the 6-3 Slaughter decision overturned Humphrey's Executor for other agencies but explicitly reaffirmed Fed independence as structural principle. Justice Kavanaugh warned: "even temporary uncertainty about the status of the Federal Reserve could spark political upheaval...as well as turmoil in the U.S. and world economies." This escalation from political messaging to prosecution threat represented unprecedented institutional pressure, revealing that equity-market protection and monetary accommodation ranked above Fed independence in the administration's revealed preferences. Yet the Fed held; rate hikes proceeded despite prosecution threat.

The tariff regime itself persisted through legal setback: Supreme Court struck down IEEPA-based tariffs in February 2026; administration immediately pivoted to 10% Section 122 tariff maintaining USMCA exemptions, then revised upward to 15% within 24 hours. The July 1, 2026 USMCA renewal deadline loomed as the next critical juncture—absent clean extension, annual reviews would extend uncertainty into 2036. Administration credibility on trade execution remained ambiguous: the threatened 10% Canadian fall-2025 tariff never materialized; the 100% auto threat remained unimplemented. Yet USMCA-compliant imports surged 45 percentage points in nine months (44% compliant in 2024 to 89% in October 2025), demonstrating that importers internalized exemption arbitrage and restructured supply flows despite ambiguous commitment durability.
